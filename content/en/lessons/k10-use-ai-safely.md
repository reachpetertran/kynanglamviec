## 3. Explanation

Generative AI tools, such as chatbots, help you draft emails, summarise documents and suggest ideas. Used well, they save a lot of time. But there are two main risks: **leaking information** and **wrong output**.

**Risk 1: leaking information.** What you paste into an external AI tool may be stored outside your organisation. Before pasting, ask yourself: "Am I allowed to send this outside?" Usually, do **not** put in:

- Personal data of customers or colleagues: names with phone numbers, addresses, ID numbers.
- Internal documents: cost prices, contracts, unannounced plans.
- Passwords and access codes.

A safer way: remove sensitive details or replace them with labels, such as "Customer A". Most important: **follow your workplace's rules on AI use**. If there are none yet, ask your manager first.

**Risk 2: wrong output.** AI can be wrong while sounding very confident. It can invent figures, document names and links. Before using its output, check with 3 questions:

<div class="flow" markdown="1">
<span>Correct?</span>
<span>Complete?</span>
<span>Appropriate?</span>
</div>

1. **Correct?** Do the figures, names, dates and rules match the original source? Check the source yourself.
2. **Complete?** Has it left out anything important from the original?
3. **Appropriate?** Do the tone and form of address suit the reader and your workplace culture?

**You are still responsible.** Anything you send is yours, even if AI drafted it. Do not send anything you have not read carefully.

## 4. Workplace example

> **Hypothetical scenario.** Clara is new in the HR team of a garment factory. She needs to write a notice about annual health checks for 200 workers. The factory allows one AI tool but bans entering personal data.

Clara does not paste the worker list. She only types: "Write a short, easy notice for workers about health check times by team, with time, place and what to bring."

The AI draft says "bring your old medical record and insurance card". Clara checks with the 3 questions:

- **Correct?** The clinic only asks for an ID card, not an insurance card. Clara fixes it.
- **Complete?** It misses the clinic's instruction "do not eat breakfast". Clara adds it.
- **Appropriate?** The wording is rather formal. Clara makes it friendlier.

**Why this works:** Clara does not send personal data outside. She also checks the draft against the clinic's real requirements.

## 5. Practice

> **Hypothetical scenario.** You work in customer service. You want AI to summarise last week's 50 customer complaint messages. Each message includes the customer's name and phone number.

Write (1) how you would prepare the data before using AI, (2) how you would check the summary.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

First: do your company's rules allow this? If so, what should you remove from the messages?
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

To check "correct" and "complete", you can read a few random messages yourself and compare them with the summary.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Model answer</summary>

1. Check the company's AI rules, or ask your team leader. If allowed, remove names and phone numbers, keeping only the complaint text.
2. Read 10 random messages and check their main issues appear in the summary. Check every number the AI gives, such as "15 customers complained of slow delivery", by counting yourself.

(The number of messages is hypothetical.)
</details>
