## 3. Explanation

Major decisions are usually discussed by the leadership team. A group brings more views, but it also has three traps.

- **Optimism:** plans always look faster and cheaper than reality.
- **Deference to the most senior person:** when the boss speaks first, everyone follows.
- **Groupthink:** people with doubts hesitate to speak.

**Tool 1. The outside view.** Do not estimate only from your own plan. Ask: how long and how much did similar projects really take? This is the idea behind *reference class forecasting*.

<figure class="fig"><img src="../../../assets/img/en/n6/l2-two-views.svg" alt="Two views when estimating the time to open 20 shops, hypothetical. Inside view: the plan says 6 months. Outside view: 6 previous expansions took 8 to 11 months, with the middle at about 9 months. The base estimate should start from 9 months, then be adjusted for specific differences." width="640" height="400" loading="lazy"><figcaption><b>Figure 1.</b> Start from data on similar cases, then adjust. The figures are hypothetical.</figcaption></figure>

How to do it: find at least 5 completed cases, inside or outside the company. Look at the actual results. Use the middle value as the base estimate. Adjust only for a specific reason.

**Tool 2. A group pre-mortem.** Before you commit, the chair says: "Imagine it is a year from now and this has failed badly. Why?"

<div class="diagram"><p class="dg-title">A 45-minute pre-mortem</p><ol class="steps">
<li><b>5 minutes</b><span>State the decision and imagine it has failed.</span></li>
<li><b>5 minutes</b><span>Everyone writes reasons alone, no discussion.</span></li>
<li><b>15 minutes</b><span>Read reasons in turn. The most senior person reads last.</span></li>
<li><b>15 minutes</b><span>Choose the 3 biggest. Each gets a measure and an owner.</span></li>
<li><b>5 minutes</b><span>Write stop signals and a review date.</span></li>
</ol></div>

Writing alone before discussing stops opinions being swept along by the first speaker. The most senior person speaks last so others dare to speak.

A pre-mortem does not aim to cancel the decision. It makes the decision sturdier, with fallbacks and a stopping point.

## 4. Workplace example

> **Hypothetical scenario.** A chain of mini-supermarkets plans to open 20 new stores in a new region. The plan says 6 months.

The operations director, Nora, reviews the company's 6 previous expansions. They took 8 to 11 months. The middle is about 9 months.

The leadership team sets a base estimate of 9 months. They adjust it to 8 months only because a site-finding team is already in place.

Then Nora chairs a pre-mortem. The CEO reads their reasons last. The three biggest reasons are chosen:

1. Not enough store managers. **Measure:** train 10 deputy managers from month one. **Owner:** HR head.
2. Leases signed late. **Measure:** sign in batches of 5 sites. **Owner:** head of site development.
3. Fresh food spoils because the warehouse is far. **Measure:** test 3 stores before opening more. **Owner:** supply chain director.

**Stop signal:** if the first 3 stores lose money for more than 2 months in a row, meet again. **Review date:** end of month 3.

**Why this works:** the estimate rests on real data. Reasons for failure are voiced before committing, and each has an owner.

## 5. Practice

> **Hypothetical scenario.** You are deputy CEO of a software company. The leadership team plans to replace the accounting system in 4 months.

Do two things: (1) Explain how you would find 5 similar cases. (2) Write 3 possible reasons for failure, each with a measure and an owner.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Similar cases could be the company's past major software replacements. You could also ask the implementation partner about clients of the same size.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Think about people, data and timing. What is the risk of switching during year-end closing?
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **Reference class:** 3 software replacement projects at the company in the past 5 years; 2 clients of the same size introduced by the implementation partner. Record the actual duration of each.
2. **Reasons for failure:**
   - Old data is wrong and does not transfer cleanly. **Measure:** clean the data a month before. **Owner:** chief accountant.
   - It clashes with year-end closing. **Measure:** run both systems in parallel through one period close. **Owner:** finance director.
   - Staff are not used to it. **Measure:** role-based training, with on-site support for 2 weeks. **Owner:** project lead.

(The details are hypothetical.)
</details>

**Level of evidence.**
Outside view: Flyvbjerg (2006), *Project Management Journal* 37(3), building on Kahneman and Tversky's decision theory.
Pre-mortem: Klein (2007), *Harvard Business Review*, September 2007. This is a practice article, not peer-reviewed research.
The experimental basis for the pre-mortem is Mitchell, Russo and Pennington (1989), *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making* 2(1). It found that people give more reasons when they treat an outcome as having already happened. It does not show that decisions get better.
