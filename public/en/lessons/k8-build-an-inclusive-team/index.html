Build a team with clear roles where everyone can contribute

This lesson completes Sheet 6: Team working agreement. Print template. Rights at work, such as freedom from discrimination, are not covered here. They will be covered on trachnhiemxahoi.com (in Vietnamese, coming soon). Here we learn the skill: organising a team so that everyone can contribute.

1. Objectives

  • Write a team working agreement with your team: roles, meetings, sharing work, rules for different circumstances.
  • Use 3 questions so the team reviews how it works together each quarter.

2. Retrieval warm-up

  1. What are the 6 steps for preparing a difficult conversation?
  2. When mediating between two people, why meet each alone first?
Answers
  1. Facts; impact; opening line; ask and listen; find a way together; agreement and review date.
  2. So each person can speak honestly and calm down before sitting together.

Review: Difficult conversations.

3. Explanation

An inclusive team is one where everyone knows their role and dares to speak up. This does not happen by itself. The manager has to organise the way of working so that it happens.

A working agreement has four parts. The team writes it together; the manager does not write it alone.

  1. Roles: who is responsible for what, and who decides what. When two people both think a task is theirs, or nobody's, conflict starts easily.
  2. Meetings: send documents in advance. Let people write their views down before speaking. Invite quieter people to speak first; the manager speaks last.
  3. Sharing invisible tasks: taking minutes, tidying up, welcoming newcomers, covering out of hours. Rotate them fairly; do not default to the same person.
  4. Rules for different circumstances: remote workers, shift workers, parents of young children, people who speak another language. For example: meet during hours when everyone is available, and record important decisions in writing.

Review each quarter with 3 questions. The team answers anonymously, then discusses the results together.

Three questions to review teamwork

  1. What helps us work well?Keep it.
  2. What makes it hard for someone to contribute?Fix it next quarter.
  3. One small change to tryWho leads it, when to review.

Inclusive does not mean everyone agrees. Good teams still argue hard about the work. The difference is that people argue about ideas, not attack each other.

When you share out work, choose people by ability and their wish to develop. Not by gender, age, where they come from, or who is close to the manager.

4. Workplace example

Hypothetical scenario. Kevin leads a software development team of 7 at a technology company. Two people work remotely in another city. Lena has a young child and must leave before 17:30.

In meetings, Kevin and two long-serving developers talk for most of the time. Remote colleagues often cannot hear well. Meetings often run past 18:00, so Lena often has to leave halfway.

The whole team writes Sheet 6 together. Roles: each feature has one main owner. Meetings: documents sent a day ahead, everyone writes their views in the first 5 minutes, Kevin speaks last. Invisible tasks: minute-taking rotates weekly. Different circumstances: meetings between 9:00 and 16:00, decisions recorded in the shared channel.

At the end of the quarter, the team answers the 3 questions anonymously. One remote worker writes that it is still hard to get a word in during debates. The team tries one more thing: the facilitator asks each person by name at the end of every topic.

Why this works: the whole team wrote the agreement. The rules fix real problems. The team has a way to review and keep improving.

5. Practice

Hypothetical scenario. You lead a maintenance crew of 9 at an industrial park. The crew works three shifts. Two young technicians rarely speak in meetings. Writing up the shift handover log always falls to Nora, the only woman on the crew.

Write Sheet 6 for your crew.

Hint 1

With three shifts, when can everyone meet? How does information pass between shifts?

Hint 2

The handover log is an invisible task. Why does it always fall to Nora? How could it be shared fairly?

Model answer
  • Roles: each machine area has one lead technician per shift; the crew leader decides when a machine is down for more than 2 hours.
  • Meetings: 15 minutes at the Monday morning handover, including the night shift that has just finished; invite the two young technicians to report first.
  • Invisible tasks: whoever leads the shift writes that shift's handover log; the shift lead role rotates weekly.
  • Different circumstances: the night shift gets announcements in the log and the group chat, not only by word of mouth.
  • Review: at the end of the quarter, the 3 questions on anonymous paper slips, dropped in a box in the break room.

6. Self-check

Choose an answer. Every option has an explanation.

Question 1. Who should write the team's working agreement?

  • Not quite. Rules written by one person often miss other people's real problems, and are rarely kept.

  • Correct. Correct. Rules written together fit reality better, and everyone has a reason to keep them.

  • Not quite. Company-wide rules are still needed, but each team must agree its own way of working.

Question 2. In meetings, the manager usually gives their own view first. What is the problem?

  • Not quite. It is faster, but other views are lost. Many people will not dare to disagree with the boss.

  • Correct. Correct. Inviting others first and speaking last helps the team hear more ideas.

  • Not quite. The issue is not credibility, but that the team does not hear enough views.

Question 3. Making tea and taking minutes are always given to the youngest woman on the team. What is a sensible fix?

  • Not quite. Defaulting invisible tasks by gender or age is unfair, and she loses time for her main work.

  • Correct. Correct. Rotation shares the load, and everyone sees small tasks as the whole team's work.

  • Not quite. Minutes are still needed to record decisions. The issue is who does it, not whether it is done.

Question 4. The team argues hard about how to run a project. What does this mean?

  • Not quite. Inclusive does not mean everyone agrees. Arguing about the work can lead to better decisions.

  • Correct. Correct. A team that dares to argue about the work feels safe to speak. The manager keeps it from becoming personal.

  • Not quite. Deciding too fast may miss good ideas. Hear enough first, then decide against shared criteria.

7. Reflection

  1. In your team, who is heard least in meetings? Why?
  2. Which invisible task always falls to the same person?

8. Real-world application

In the next 7 days, bring Sheet 6 to a team meeting. Fill in at least two parts together. Set a review date at the end of the quarter.
