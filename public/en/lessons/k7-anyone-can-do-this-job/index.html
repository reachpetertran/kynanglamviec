Anyone can do this job

For the adult. A gender stereotype about jobs is the belief that a job only suits boys, or only suits girls.

  • Why start early: the Drawing the Future survey (2018) asked children aged 7–11 to draw the job they wanted. The report found children's choices often followed gender stereotypes. The OECD report Dream Jobs? (2020), using PISA 2018 data, also found that gender still strongly shapes the jobs 15-year-olds hope for.
  • You need: white paper, coloured pencils, a timer.
  • Note: children learn a lot from adults' jokes. Avoid remarks like "a girl wanting to be an engineer?".
  • Equal rights at work for adults will be covered on trachnhiemxahoi.com (in Vietnamese, coming soon). This lesson stays at the level of exploring jobs.

1. Objectives

  • Spot a sentence that shows a gender stereotype about jobs.
  • Turn it into a question: "What skills does this job need?"

2. Retrieval warm-up

  1. Name 3 of the 6 skills that every job needs.
  2. What does box 5 of the Job card ask?
Answers
  1. Listening, speaking clearly, solving problems, working together, keeping promises, using technology.
  2. "What skills?"

Review: Every job needs skills.

3. Explanation

You may have heard sentences like: "Only boys become engineers." Or: "Only girls look after young children." These are called gender stereotypes about jobs.

A stereotype is a familiar idea that has not been checked. Many people repeat it because they are used to hearing it, not because it is true.

In Lesson 2, you learned that jobs need skills and knowledge. A civil engineer needs careful calculation and teamwork. A nursery teacher needs to listen to children and speak clearly. Anyone who learns and practises these can do the job.

Stereotypes narrow your choices. If you believe a job is "not for people like me", you may skip a job that suits you. The same is true for your friends.

The best way is to turn the sentence into a question:

Turn a stereotype into a question

Stereotype
  • "Girls can't be electricians."
  • "A boy being a nurse?"
  • "This job is for boys."
Opening question
  • "What skills does an electrician need?"
  • "What does a nurse do?"
  • "Would you like to find out about this job?"

4. Workplace example

Hypothetical scenario. In a careers activity lesson, a class of 8-year-olds draws "an engineer". Ben says loudly: "Engineers have to be boys!"

The teacher does not criticise Ben. She uses 4 steps:

  1. Pause: "Ben has shared an idea. Let's check it together."
  2. Ask about skills: "What does a civil engineer do? What skills do they need?" The class answers: calculating, drawing, teamwork.
  3. Find real examples: she shows photos of men and women engineers at work, from books and newspapers.
  4. Say a new sentence: the class says: "Anyone good at calculating and teamwork can be an engineer."

4 steps when you hear a stereotype

  1. PauseDon't rush to agree or to argue.
  2. Ask about skills"What skills does this job need?"
  3. Find real examplesAsk an adult to help find real people who do the job.
  4. Say a new sentence"Anyone who practises those skills can do it."

Why this works: the teacher does not scold or give a long lecture. She helps the class check an idea themselves, with questions and real examples.

5. Practice

Game: "Quick-draw a worker". The adult reads out 4 jobs: firefighter, nurse, pilot, cook. For each job, draw a person quickly, in 1 minute.

When you have finished, answer:

  1. Did you draw each worker as a man or a woman?
  2. Choose one drawing. Draw the same job again with a person of the other gender.
  3. Turn these 2 sentences into questions about skills: "Girls can't drive lorries." and "Boys can't be nursery teachers."
Hint

Use the pattern: "What skills does a … need?" or "What does a … do?"

Model answer

1 and 2. There is no right or wrong. The aim is to notice the "usual" picture in your own head.

  1. "What skills does a lorry driver need?" and "What does a nursery teacher do?"

6. Self-check

Choose an answer. Every option has an explanation.

Question 1. Which sentence is a gender stereotype about jobs?

  • Not quite. This is about the knowledge a job needs, not about men and women.

  • Correct. Correct. It gives a job to one gender without looking at skills.

  • Not quite. This is about the place of work, not a stereotype.

Question 2. Your friend says: "A boy looking after little children?" Which question could you ask instead?

  • Correct. Correct. A question about skills opens the job to everyone.

  • Not quite. This repeats the stereotype without checking whether it is true.

  • Not quite. Silence does not help your friend learn more about the job.

Question 3. Why are stereotypes about jobs harmful?

  • Not quite. Stereotypes do not change the job. They change how people choose.

  • Correct. Correct. Stereotypes narrow choices from a young age.

  • Not quite. Research shows gender stereotypes shape the jobs children dream of.

Question 4. Review of Lesson 2. Which is a skill every job needs?

  • Correct. Correct. Every job needs you to hear what other people need.

  • Not quite. This is knowledge for pilots only.

  • Not quite. This is knowledge for hairdressers only.

7. Reflection

The adult asks, and you answer out loud. The site saves nothing.

  1. Is there a job you once thought was "not for me"? Why did you think so?
  2. If a friend is teased for liking a job, what could you say?

8. Real-world application

Over the next 7 days, find 2 real examples with an adult: people doing a job that is different from the usual idea. They can be people you know, or people in books or newspapers. Make a Job card for that job. You do not need to write the person's name.

Badge 3: Stereotype breaker

Connecting ideas. The key to this lesson is in Lesson 2: jobs need skills. In Lesson 6, when you interview someone, you can ask one more question: "Do both men and women do your job?" In Lesson 4, you will share group tasks by ability, not by boy or girl.
