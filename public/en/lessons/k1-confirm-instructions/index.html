Take instructions and confirm them before you start

15 min · Group N2, N3 · Updated 2026-11-14 · Bản tiếng Việt

1. Objectives

  • Ask about the three points most often missing when you get a task: result, deadline, quality standard.
  • Sum up an instruction in 1–2 sentences for the person who gave it to confirm.

2. Retrieval warm-up

This is the first lesson in the series, so the warm-up asks about your own experience. Answer in your head before reading on.

  1. The last time you did something differently from what was asked, which part was misunderstood?
  2. Which one question would have helped you avoid that mistake?

3. Explanation

The person giving a task usually has a clear picture in their head. But when they say it, they often leave out details. The person receiving it then guesses the missing parts. So the two people imagine two different results.

A simple way to prevent this: confirm before you start. There are four steps.

1. Listen fully 2. Ask about gaps 3. Sum it up 4. Wait for a yes

Step 1. Listen fully. Do not interrupt, and do not rush to say "Got it". Note the main points.

Step 2. Ask about the gaps. Check these three points. Ask about any that are unclear.

Point Suggested question
Result "When it's done, what should I give you? A file, a table, or the goods on the shelf?"
Deadline "What time and which day do you need it?"
Quality standard "What counts as done? Is there an example I can follow?"

Do not ask again about points that were already clear. Ask briefly, once, with all your questions together.

Step 3. Sum it up. Say the instruction back in your own words, in 1–2 sentences. For example: "So, I'll do X, send it to you as Y, before Z. Is that right?"

Your own words matter more than repeating word for word. When you rephrase, both of you can see where your understanding differs.

Step 4. Wait for confirmation. Only start when the other person says "Yes" or corrects you. If the task came by message, send your summary by message. Then you both have the same record.

Note: confirming is not a lack of confidence. It is a professional habit that saves both people time.

4. Workplace example

Hypothetical scenario. Lina works part-time at a café. The shift manager says: "There's a group booking tomorrow afternoon. Can you get things ready for me?"

Lina notices all three points are missing. She asks briefly:

"Can I check a few things? How many people, and what time do they arrive? What should I set up: join tables, napkins, or drinks ready? Does it need to be done by a certain time?"

The manager replies: 12 people, arriving at 15:00, join 3 tables, put out water, ready by 14:30.

Lina sums up:

"So: join 3 tables for 12 people, put water out, all ready by 14:30 tomorrow. Is that right?"

Why this works: Lina asks once and covers all three points. Her summary is short, with numbers and an exact time. The manager only needs to say "Yes".

5. Practice

Hypothetical scenario. You are a new quality control (QC) checker in a garment workshop. The line supervisor says: "Check this batch of shirts and let me know."

Write (1) the questions you need to ask and (2) the summary you would use to get confirmation.

Hint 1

How many shirts are in the batch? Check all of them or a sample? Does "let me know" mean telling him, or filling in a form?

Hint 2

The deadline and the quality standard are still missing. Check against what: seams, sizes, stains? Which faults mean rejection?

Model answer

Questions: "Should I check the whole batch or a sample? Which checklist should I use? What time do you need the result, and should I tell you or fill in the form?"

Summary: "So: I check all 200 shirts in the batch against QC checklist 2, record faults on the form, and give it to you before 16:00 today. Is that right?"

(The numbers in the model answer are hypothetical.)

6. Self-check

Choose an answer. Every option has an explanation.

Question 1. Hypothetical scenario. Your manager messages: "Could you put together this week's order summary for me?" What is missing?

  • Not quite. The result is fairly clear: an order summary table. Something else is still missing.

  • Correct. Correct. You do not know when it is needed, or which columns and template to use.

  • Not quite. If you guess the deadline and the layout, you may have to redo it.

Question 2. Which summary lets the other person confirm fastest?

  • Not quite. It does not show how you understood the task, so nobody can spot a gap.

  • Correct. Correct. Short, with the result, deadline and standard, ending with a question.

  • Not quite. Repeating word for word does not show you understood. Rephrasing reveals any gaps.

Question 3. Hypothetical scenario. A customer says clearly: "5 rice boxes to take away, collecting at 11:30." What should you do?

  • Not quite. The customer has said everything. Asking again about clear points wastes time and annoys people.

  • Correct. Correct. Ask only about gaps. When everything is clear, a short summary is enough.

  • Not quite. A summary takes seconds but catches mishearings, such as 5 becoming 15.

Question 4. The task was given by chat message. Which way of confirming fits best?

  • Not quite. A call is fine, but also send a summary message so you both have the same record.

  • Correct. Correct. The summary message is a shared record you can check later if questions arise.

  • Not quite. An emoji only shows you read it, not how you understood it.

7. Reflection

  1. In the past week, did you start a task while one of the three points was still unclear? What happened?
  2. Which summary sentence will you use in your own work?

8. Real-world application

In the next 7 days, use the four steps for the first task you are given: listen fully, ask about gaps, sum up, wait for confirmation. Note the result in your evidence journal: what you asked, and how the other person reacted.
