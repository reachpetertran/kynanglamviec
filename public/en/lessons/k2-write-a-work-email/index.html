Write a work email with one purpose and one clear request

15 min · Group N2, N3 · Updated 2026-11-14 · Bản tiếng Việt

1. Objectives

  • Write a subject line that says what it is about and what you need.
  • Write the email body in 4 parts: greeting, context, request, deadline.

2. Retrieval warm-up

Answer first, then open the answers.

  1. What are the columns of a 5-column task list?
  2. What are the 4 parts of an early-warning message?
Answers
  1. Task, result, deadline, given by, status.
  2. Status, reason, options, request.

Not sure? Review Track your tasks and flag delays early.

3. Explanation

People read emails very quickly. Many read only the subject line and the first two lines. So the most important information must come first.

Rule 1: one email, one purpose. If you have two different matters, write two emails. Mixing several matters makes it easy to miss one.

Rule 2: the subject says what it is about and what you need. Compare:

Weak Better
Quick question Please approve the flyer print quote – by 15:00 Thursday
Report Week 46 sales report – no reply needed

Add "no reply needed" when the email is just for information. The reader then knows there is nothing to do.

Rule 3: write the body in 4 parts.

Greeting Context Request Deadline

  1. Greeting: one line, with the right form of address.
  2. Context: 1–2 sentences, enough to understand without searching old emails.
  3. Request: say clearly what you need the reader to do. One sentence, with a verb: approve, send, choose, confirm.
  4. Deadline: when you need it, and why if that helps.

End with your name. If you attach a file, say so in the body: "The quote is attached."

Before you click Send, check quickly:

  • Are the recipients and the people in CC (carbon copy, sent for information) correct?
  • Is the file attached?
  • Reading only the subject and the request, would the reader know what to do?

4. Workplace example

Hypothetical scenario. Maya is a marketing intern at a small company. She needs her team leader to approve a quote for printing 2,000 flyers. The printer holds the price until the end of Thursday.

Maya's email:

Subject: Please approve the flyer print quote – by 15:00 Thursday

Hi Ben,

I've got a quote for printing 2,000 flyers for the opening event. The printer holds this price until the end of Thursday.

Could you approve the attached quote, or tell me if anything needs changing?

If you can approve it by 15:00 Thursday, I can place the order the same day.

Maya

Why this works: The subject alone tells Ben the task and the deadline. The request uses the verb "approve". The reason for the deadline is clear.

5. Practice

Hypothetical scenario. You work in an admin team. You need department heads to send you their lists of attendees for a safety training on the 28th. You need the lists by 17:00 on the 21st to book a room.

Write the subject and body.

Hint 1

The subject needs: what it is about (attendee list), what you need (send it), and the deadline (17:00 on the 21st).

Hint 2

The request should say the format: full name and department, by replying to this email.

Model answer

Subject: Send attendee list for safety training on the 28th – by 17:00 on the 21st

Dear department heads,

On the 28th, the company is running a safety training. The admin team needs numbers to book a room.

Please reply to this email with the full names of attendees from your department.

The deadline is 17:00 on the 21st, so I can book the room on the 22nd.

Anna, Admin team

(Names and dates are hypothetical.)

6. Self-check

Choose an answer. Every option has an explanation.

Question 1. Which is the best subject line for an email asking for one day of leave?

  • Not quite. The reader does not know what you are asking for, or when.

  • Correct. Correct. It has the topic (leave), a specific date and the deadline for approval.

  • Not quite. It is too long and still does not say what the reader needs to do.

Question 2. You need to ask accounts two different things: the invoice deadline and how to fill in an advance form. What should you do?

  • Not quite. With two matters in one email, one often goes unanswered.

  • Correct. Correct. One purpose per email helps the reader handle and answer each one fully.

  • Not quite. A call is possible, but this lesson is about writing clear emails.

Question 3. Which request is clearest?

  • Not quite. "Consider" does not say what to do: approve, change, or just read.

  • Correct. Correct. It has a specific verb (choose), the choices and a deadline.

  • Not quite. It is not clear what response is needed, or when.

Question 4. The email only announces a changed meeting time. What helps readers most?

  • Correct. Correct. Readers know straight away they only need to read it.

  • Not quite. Many will reply "Received", filling everyone's inbox.

  • Not quite. An importance flag does not say whether a reply is needed.

7. Reflection

  1. Could the subject line of your last work email be understood on its own?
  2. Did the request in that email have a clear verb? If not, how would you rewrite it?

8. Real-world application

In the next 7 days, for the first three work emails you send, check: does the subject have the topic and deadline, and does the request have a verb? Note one email you changed before sending.
