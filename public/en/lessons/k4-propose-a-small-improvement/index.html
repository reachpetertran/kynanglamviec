Write a small improvement proposal with the current situation, solution and benefits

20 min · Group N3 · Updated 2026-11-14 · Bản tiếng Việt

1. Objectives

  • Write a 1-page improvement proposal with: current situation, solution, benefits, trial.
  • Propose one small trial, with a way to measure the result.

2. Retrieval warm-up

  1. What are the 3 stages of the first 90 days, and the focus of each?
  2. Why agree your plan with your manager in the first week?
Answers
  1. Learn (days 1–30), do it yourself (31–60), contribute (61–90).
  2. So you and your manager have the same expectations.

Review: Plan your first 90 days.

3. Explanation

You see a task that could be done faster, with fewer errors. Saying "I think we should do it differently" rarely leads anywhere. A short, structured proposal is far more likely to be accepted.

A 1-page proposal has 4 parts:

Current situation Solution Benefits Trial

  1. Current situation: how it is done now and what the problem is, with numbers. "About 40 minutes a day spent looking for files" is clearer than "finding files takes ages".
  2. Solution: what exactly you propose. Include the cost or effort it needs.
  3. Benefits: what it saves, which errors it reduces, for whom. Keep estimates reasonable, and label them as estimates.
  4. Trial: a small trial first: what to measure, and for how long.

Why a small trial? A manager agrees more easily to two weeks at one counter than to changing the whole store. If it does not work, the cost is low. If it works, you have real data to scale up.

Measure before and after. Before the trial, measure the current situation. After the trial, measure again in the same way. Without a "before" measure, you cannot prove an improvement.

Three things to avoid:

  • Criticising the old way or the people who do it. Focus on the process, not people.
  • Proposing something too big the first time. Start small, within your own work.
  • Ignoring the people who do the work. Ask them before you write the proposal.

4. Workplace example

Hypothetical scenario. Mira is a new accounts assistant at a trading company. At the end of each month, the accounts team spends a long time matching invoices to orders.

Mira's proposal:

Current situation: at month end, 2 people spend about 3 days matching around 400 invoices. Invoices are filed by date received, not by order number. In October, 12 invoices were matched wrongly.

Solution: when an invoice arrives, enter its order number straight away in a new column of the tracking spreadsheet. This takes about 30 seconds more per invoice.

Benefits (estimated): month-end matching cut to about 1.5 days; fewer matching errors.

Trial: use it in December for invoices from our largest supplier. Measure matching time and errors, and compare with November.

Why this works: it has figures for the current situation, states the extra effort, and proposes a small trial with a measure.

5. Practice

Hypothetical scenario. You are a waiter at a restaurant. You notice customers often have to call staff 2–3 times before someone comes, because some tables are in a hidden corner. You think of a small call button on each table.

Write a 4-part proposal of at most 8 lines.

Hint 1

Current situation: you need numbers. You could count over 3 shifts: how often did customers have to call more than once?

Hint 2

Trial: buttons on how many tables, for how long, measuring what?

Model answer

Current situation: over the last 3 evening shifts, I counted 14 times when customers at the 4 corner tables had to call 2 or more times.

Solution: put wireless call buttons on the 4 corner tables. The cost needs a real price check.

Benefits (estimated): customers do not have to keep calling; fewer complaints.

Trial: 2 weeks at the 4 corner tables. Count again how often customers have to call more than once, and compare with the 3 earlier shifts.

(The figures are hypothetical.)

6. Self-check

Choose an answer. Every option has an explanation.

Question 1. Which sentence fits the "Current situation" part best?

  • Not quite. This is a personal opinion with no numbers, and it can upset people.

  • Correct. Correct. It has specific numbers and a clear problem.

  • Not quite. It criticises a person instead of describing the process.

Question 2. Why run a small trial before rolling out widely?

  • Correct. Correct. A small trial lowers risk and is easier for a manager to approve.

  • Not quite. The aim is to test, not to delay.

  • Not quite. Not true. A small trial shows early whether the improvement works.

Question 3. You try a new method for 2 weeks but forgot to measure before. What is the problem?

  • Not quite. Without "before" data, there is nothing to compare with.

  • Correct. Correct. You need to measure before and after in the same way.

  • Not quite. No need to drop it. You can measure the old method for a while to compare.

Question 4. Before writing a proposal to change how the warehouse is arranged, what should you do?

  • Correct. Correct. They know the real difficulties and may suggest a better solution.

  • Not quite. Skipping your manager and the people doing the work makes support less likely.

  • Not quite. Changing things without permission can cause confusion and goes beyond your authority.

7. Reflection

  1. In your current work, which task is repetitive, slow or often goes wrong?
  2. Do you have any numbers about it? How could you measure it?

8. Real-world application

In the next 7 days, choose a small task you want to improve. Measure the current situation at least 3 times, then write a 1-page proposal in 4 parts. If possible, send it to your manager and note their response.
