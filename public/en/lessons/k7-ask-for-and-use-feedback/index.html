Ask for feedback and turn it into action

15 min · Group N3 · Updated 2026-11-14 · Bản tiếng Việt

1. Objectives

  • Ask one specific feedback question about a task you have just done.
  • Turn a piece of feedback into one action with a deadline.

2. Retrieval warm-up

  1. What are the 4 points when describing a problem?
  2. When should you report at once, without stopping to think of options?
Answers
  1. What happened, when, the impact, what you have tried.
  2. When there is a safety incident.

Review: Describe a problem and bring two options.

3. Explanation

Feedback is information others give you about how you work. New people improve fast when they get feedback often. But many wait for their formal review to hear it. By then it is late.

Step 1. Ask specifically, and ask early. "How am I doing?" usually gets "Fine". Ask about one specific task you have just finished:

  • "In that presentation, which part should I have made shorter?"
  • "In this report, is there one thing I should do differently next time?"

Asking for "one thing" makes it easier for people to answer.

Step 2. Listen and clarify; do not defend yourself. When you get feedback, just say thank you and ask for detail: "Could you give me an example of where?" If you think the feedback is wrong, note it and think about it later. Do not argue at that moment.

Step 3. Turn it into action.

Feedback Specific action Deadline Report back

For example, the feedback is "Your reports are too long."

  • Action: next week's report has at most 7 lines.
  • Deadline: next Friday.
  • Report back: "I've shortened it based on your feedback. Could you take a look?"

Reporting back matters. The person sees their feedback was useful, so they are glad to give more next time.

Keep a record. Keep a list of feedback and actions. It is evidence of your progress at review time.

4. Workplace example

Hypothetical scenario. Ryan is a new technician at a phone repair centre. He has just dealt with a customer complaint.

Ryan asks his shift leader: "With that last customer, is there one thing I should do differently?"

Shift leader: "You explained too much technical detail; the customer didn't follow. Give the result first."

Ryan asks: "How should I start, for example?" The shift leader suggests: "Your phone is fixed. The problem was…"

Ryan writes in his notebook: Feedback: give the result first. Action: start with the result for the next 5 customers. Deadline: end of the week. Report back to Mark on Saturday.

Why this works: the question is specific, Ryan asks for detail instead of defending himself, and the action is measurable.

5. Practice

Hypothetical scenario. You work in phone customer service. Your team leader says: "You often leave customers waiting a long time without saying anything."

Write (1) a question to clarify the feedback, (2) one action with a deadline, (3) your report-back message.

Hint 1

Clarify: how long is "a long time"? What should you say when you put a customer on hold?

Hint 2

The action should be measurable, for example: tell the customer whenever a wait will be more than 30 seconds.

Model answer
  1. "Roughly how long before a customer feels it's too long, and what should I say then?"
  2. "From tomorrow until Friday, when a lookup takes over 30 seconds, I'll tell the customer and give a time."
  3. "This week I've told customers whenever they had to wait. Could you listen to a few calls and give me more feedback?"

(The 30-second mark is hypothetical.)

6. Self-check

Choose an answer. Every option has an explanation.

Question 1. Which feedback question is most likely to get useful feedback?

  • Not quite. The question is too general and usually gets "Fine".

  • Correct. Correct. It is about one specific, recent task, and asks for just "one thing".

  • Not quite. The end of the year is too late to fix things in time.

Question 2. Hypothetical scenario. You get feedback you think is wrong. What should you do at that moment?

  • Not quite. Defending yourself at once can make people reluctant to give feedback next time.

  • Correct. Correct. You can still discuss it later, once you have thought it through.

  • Not quite. Ignoring it means missing the chance to understand another view.

Question 3. Feedback: "Your emails are a bit hard to follow." Which action is most measurable?

  • Not quite. "Try" cannot be measured, so it is hard to know if you did it.

  • Correct. Correct. It has specific criteria and a time frame, so you can check it yourself.

  • Not quite. It may help, but it is not tied to the behaviour to change or to a deadline.

Question 4. Why report back to the person who gave you feedback?

  • Correct. Correct. Reporting back also lets them confirm you changed in the right direction.

  • Not quite. The main purpose is to close the feedback loop, not to get praise.

  • Not quite. If you do not report back, they may not notice the change.

7. Reflection

  1. What was the last feedback you received? What did you do with it?
  2. Who could you ask, about which task, next week?

8. Real-world application

In the next 7 days, ask for feedback once about a specific task you have just done. Record the feedback, the action, the deadline and the date you reported back.
